# How to annotate: AP Lang, AP Lit and college reading

Drawn from teacher and writing-centre guides (sources at the end), turned into rules for the engine. Read this before
planning any annotations.

## The one rule that matters most

Annotation is **showing your thinking while you read**, like showing your work in math. A mark with no thought behind
it is a scavenger hunt. Spotting alliteration is useless unless you say what it does. So:

- Nearly every mark gets a short note saying **why it matters**: the effect, the meaning, the question it raises.
  Vocabulary circles are the exception: their note is the definition.
- Use the formula **label + effect**: "anaphora → urgency", "gray x3 = dull, trapped", "shift: hope → despair",
  "irony: she sells hair, he sells watch".
- Talk back to the text: ask questions, agree or disagree, react ("!! didn't see that coming"), predict, connect.

## How much

- Sparingly. If every line is marked, nothing stands out. About 2 to 5 marks per page in prose, more in a short poem.
- Underline a few words, never a whole passage. For a whole passage use a `bracket` or a `vline` down the margin.
- Highlight key information only. Default to plain yellow. Use colour coding only if Niko asks for it, and then keep
  one meaning per colour and say what the key is.
- Stars are rare: the handful of most important statements in the whole text.

## Default key (map to the engine's marks)

| Meaning | Mark | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Key idea, key line | `highlight` (yellow) | what it means, or why it is key |
| Key word or phrase | `underline` | always with a comment |
| Word to look up | `circle` | the definition, short |
| Important passage | `bracket` | what the passage does |
| Very important / whole paragraph | `vline` (or `double-bracket`) | optional |
| Most important statements (rare) | `star` | optional |
| I understand / I agree | `check` | none needed |
| Confusing | `mark: "none"` or underline | `?` or a real question ("why does she lie here?") |
| Surprising, new | | `!` plus what surprised |
| Steps of an argument | | numbers `1`, `2`, `3` in the margin, one note per point |
| Connected ideas, repeats, contrasts | `link_to` (arrow from one quote to another) | what connects them |
| Section or chapter summary | page note, `"at": "top"` | a few words of what happens or what is argued |
| Overall theme or takeaway | page note, `"at": "bottom"` | one sentence |

Literary shorthand in the margin (always add a few words): **S** symbol, **I** imagery, **F** figurative language,
**T** tone, **Th** theme, **D** diction, **Syn** syntax, **Ch** characterization, **Fs** foreshadowing.

## What to look for

**AP Lang (nonfiction, speeches, essays: rhetoric)**
- SOAPSTone, usually as a top or bottom page note: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject, Tone.
- The claim or thesis (often a bracket or star), and each piece of evidence: what type it is (anecdote, statistic,
  expert, example, history) and what it does for the claim.
- Appeals: ethos, logos, pathos, and how the writer builds them ("ethos: veteran, so we trust him").
- Rhetorical choices and their effect: diction, syntax (short sentences, parallelism, anaphora, rhetorical questions),
  figurative language, juxtaposition, concession and rebuttal, absolute language ("never", "all"), numbers.
- **Shifts**: "but", "however", "yet", a change in tone or topic. Mark them and name the before and after.
- Number the moves of the argument in the margin when it builds point by point.

**AP Lit (fiction)**
- Characterization and change: what a choice or detail reveals, and how the character is different from before.
- Narrator and point of view, and whether the narrator is reliable.
- Patterns: repeated words, images and events (motifs), and where the pattern breaks. Connect repeats with `link_to`.
- Symbols, imagery, figurative language, irony, tone and mood, and where they shift.
- Setting, conflict, structure and pacing, allusions.
- Title: note what it might mean at the start, and come back to it at the end.
- Theme: implied, a claim about life or people, not one word ("love = sacrifice", not just "love").

**Poetry**
- React beside the title before reading, and revisit it after.
- Speaker and situation; diction (especially verbs and nouns, general vs specific); imagery and sound.
- Line breaks and enjambment, form, punctuation that makes you pause.
- The turn (volta) or shift: mark it and say what changes.

**College reading (textbooks, articles)**
- Main idea of each section, in your own words, as a top note (a running outline).
- Definitions and key terms (circle and define), and the author's main points and shifts in argument.
- Your questions and objections, and links to class or other texts.

## Process

1. First read for the gist, context and purpose. Write a one-line purpose or summary.
2. Second read: mark the significant choices, with notes.
3. After reading: summarize at the top or bottom of the section, return to the title, note patterns that emerged.

When planning for Niko: do the reading and the analysis, then turn it into a plan that follows these rules. Keep every
note short enough for the margin (1 to 6 words); longer thoughts go into a top or bottom page note.
